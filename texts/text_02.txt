# Linear Algebra for Deep Learning

If you had to choose one area of mathematics to understand deep learning, it would be linear algebra.

Calculus explains how neural networks learn. Probability describes uncertainty and loss. Optimization determines how parameters are updated. Linear algebra describes what the network actually does: a sequence of transformations that mix representations, followed by pointwise nonlinearities.

At the core are a few concepts: **vectors, vector spaces, basis, dimension, inner products, linear transformations, matrices, and matrix multiplication**.

A vector is not fundamentally an arrow or an array of numbers. It is an element of a vector space. In deep learning, embeddings, activations, gradients, signals, and model parameters can all be treated as vectors.

A **basis** defines how vectors are represented. Coordinates depend on the basis; the underlying vector does not. This perspective explains why the DFT can be understood as a change of basis, and why representations learned by different models cannot necessarily be compared coordinate-by-coordinate.

**Dimension** is the number of independent degrees of freedom available to represent something. Embedding size, hidden size, and latent size are all manifestations of this idea. Dimension is a representational budget: more capacity generally means more memory and computation.

A vector space alone does not tell us what “distance,” “angle,” or “orthogonality” means. An **inner product** adds that geometry. From it we obtain norms, cosine similarity, orthogonality, and projection. These concepts appear directly in speaker embeddings, SI-SDR, beamforming, and many optimization methods.

A **linear transformation** preserves linear combinations. A matrix is simply the coordinate representation of such a transformation after choosing bases. This is why it is more useful to think of a matrix as an operation rather than a table of numbers.

**Matrix multiplication is composition.**
Applying \(B\) after \(A\) gives \(BA\). This explains non-commutativity, shape constraints, and why changing the order of operations can radically change computational cost.

This viewpoint unifies much of modern AI.

A neural layer is essentially a linear or affine transformation plus a nonlinearity. Convolution can be viewed as a structured matrix operation. Attention consists largely of learned linear projections and inner products. STFT is a change into the Fourier basis. Mel filtering is another linear transformation. Beamforming operates through covariance matrices, projections, and subspaces. Quantized inference is largely matrix multiplication performed with lower-precision arithmetic.

The practical lesson is simple: **stop treating linear algebra as a collection of formulas to memorize. Treat it as the language for reading models, code, and papers.**

When you see `nn.Linear`, attention, a covariance matrix, an embedding, or a shape mismatch, ask the same questions:

**What space am I in? What basis am I using? What transformation is being applied? What information is preserved, compressed, or discarded?**

Once those questions become intuitive, much of deep learning stops looking like a collection of unrelated tricks and starts looking like the same mathematical machinery in different forms.
