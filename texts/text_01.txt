# When an LLM Doesn’t Understand the Customer but Still Designs the Product

There is a failure mode in AI-assisted engineering that I find more dangerous than ordinary hallucination.

An LLM does not have to invent an API.

It can do something much worse:

**build an entire product around a customer flow that was never proven to exist.**

I recently worked on a highly ambitious voice project: microphone arrays, beamforming, Target Speaker Extraction (TSE), beacons, headsets, ultrasonic speakers, NPU acceleration, synchronization, and multi-station coordination.

The specification was already at version 3. The architecture looked mature: protocols, interfaces, state machines, compute budgets, synchronization logic—the works.

But when I traced the system backward from the customer, one basic question appeared:

**Where does the customer’s voiceprint come from?**

TSE needs a target representation: enrollment audio, a speaker embedding, reference audio, or some other conditioning signal. The system must know **whose voice it is trying to extract**.

That is not an NPU problem. It is not a Qualcomm problem. It is a **product-flow problem**.

The architecture may say:

Microphone → Beamforming → TSE → Speaker Identification

But the customer flow must answer:

Who is the target?
How does the system know?
Where does the reference voice come from?
When is it enrolled?
How is it associated with the session?

Without those answers, the algorithm may be correct while the product is fundamentally wrong.

Then the architecture starts generating its own requirements.

Voiceprints require sharing. Sharing leads to a beacon. The beacon needs a protocol. The protocol needs a coordinator. The coordinator needs a roster. The roster creates a “64 voices” requirement. That drives NPU scheduling, memory architecture, and compute budgets.

Soon, every component becomes justification for another component.

The system becomes internally coherent—but externally unvalidated.

That is the dangerous part of LLM-assisted engineering.

LLMs are very good at understanding components and dependencies. They are much worse at challenging whether the underlying customer reality exists.

**System coherence is not product validity.**

A useful rule is simple:

Every feature must answer four questions:

**Who needs it?
When do they use it?
What information does the system have at that moment?
What measurable outcome proves it works?**

And one more:

**What assumption, if removed, would make this feature unnecessary?**

LLMs can make specification generation dramatically cheaper.

They do not make product validation cheaper by the same amount.

So the real workflow should be:

**Customer problem → User flow → System requirement → Constraints → Algorithm → Architecture**

Not:

**Technology → Feature → Architecture**

The most dangerous AI failure is not writing the wrong function.

It is helping us build, very efficiently, **a product that never existed in the first place.**
